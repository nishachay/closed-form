The irrationality exponent of π is 2

Fractions can never get unusually close to π.

Every number can be approached by fractions. For most numbers, the closest fractions miss by about 1 divided by the square of their bottom number. This paper proves that π behaves like most numbers. No fraction gets much closer to π than that rule allows.

The story

In 1953, Kurt Mahler proved that the irrationality exponent of π is at most 42. Since then, mathematicians made this limit smaller step by step. Salikhov reached about 7.61 in 2008. Zeilberger and Zudilin reached about 7.10 in 2020. Most experts expected the true value to be 2, but no one could prove it.

Why it matters

The irrationality exponent measures how well fractions can approximate a number. A value of 2 means π is not special in this way. The paper also settles a well-known puzzle: the Flint Hills series, the sum of 1 / (n³ sin²n). Alekseyev showed in 2011 that this sum converges if the exponent is small enough. This result is small enough.

Where it leads

The same methods could apply to other constants, such as log 2 or ζ(3), where the known limits are still far above 2. Family 017 is one of 10 results with a published reasoning summary, so mathematicians can study how the model found the proof.

What it says about AI

Strong human experts worked on this problem for decades. If the proof holds, it shows that AI can finish a long line of human work on a classical question. The repository does not list a formal Lean proof for the main paper yet.
